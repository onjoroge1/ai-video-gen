Paul Carlson's freezer is full. It's full of lionfish he hunted himself. He says they taste better than grouper. Given the choice, he'd take one over a lobster. In 2025, this Palm Beach County diver removed 1,542 of them. That made him Florida's recreational Lionfish King. Florida wants more hunters like him. But NOAA researchers have reached an awkward conclusion. These fish cannot be wiped out by conventional methods. So if Florida can never eat its way out, what is all the hunting and eating actually for? Carlson is honest about the strange part. He loves killing lionfish, even though he finds them beautiful. He admits that sounds horrible. That beauty is probably how they got here. The most likely route into the Atlantic was the aquarium trade. Unwanted pets were probably dumped into the sea. Once loose, lionfish larvae drifted on ocean currents. By 2010, they had settled from North Carolina to Bermuda to Venezuela. Nobody could scoop that back up. Spread alone would just be a curiosity. What matters is what they do to a reef. In one experiment, some small reefs each got a single lionfish. Over five weeks, young native fish settling there dropped by 79 percent. Ecologist Mark Hixon's team had to abandon some reef studies. In his words, the lionfish had moved in and were eating everything. Lionfish could also thin out parrotfish and other grazers. Those grazers keep seaweed from smothering coral. Here, lionfish have few natural predators. A female releases two egg masses of twelve to fifteen thousand eggs each. In warm water, she can spawn every four days. Florida's answer was to make people the predator. Lionfish rarely take a hook, but spears and hand nets work. No recreational license is needed with a pole spear or hand net. The spines are venomous, but the flesh is not. NOAA launched an "Eat Lionfish" campaign with fishers, wholesalers and chefs. Its scientists agreed a market was one of the only ways to cut their numbers substantially. Every summer, Florida's Lionfish Challenge turns the hunt into a contest. In 2025, its tenth year, more than 500 divers removed over 30,000 lionfish. Carlson's 1,542 came from 73 dive trips. It sounds like a plan to get rid of them. But complete eradication is likely impossible. One model asked what it would take just to stop the population growing. Its answer was to remove about 27 percent of mature lionfish, every month, for a year. That only holds them steady. Even the dinner plate has limits. Commercial profit margins turned out to be relatively low. That may be why many commercial fishers don't target lionfish. And the better the harvest works, the worse it pays. Fewer fish means lower catch rates. The densest lionfish often sit past 130 feet. Reaching them takes trained, experienced divers. In many countries, diver culling stays on reefs shallower than 30 meters. Deeper spawning populations are left largely untouched. Then came a third limit: food safety. Of 194 lionfish tested, 26 percent topped the FDA's illness threshold for ciguatoxin. That toxin can cause a dangerous food poisoning. The FDA turned wary of "Eat Lionfish." Yet as of that report, no confirmed U.S. ciguatera case had involved lionfish. So a market alone is shallow, self-limiting, and shadowed by a safety question, even an unproven one. Is the whole effort pointless? An experiment in the Bahamas suggests not. Some reefs were cleared of lionfish entirely. Others were only thinned. Thinning brought similar gains for native fish, and took 30 percent less time. Where lionfish were kept below the level where they overeat prey, native prey fish grew by fifty to seventy percent. The cuts needed varied by reef, and local monitoring can set a target for each one. So the goal isn't to finish the lionfish. It's to keep them thin. On test reefs in the Bahamas, that was enough. The lionfish were still there. But the fish breed fast. Ease off, and they quickly return. So the work has no end date. Florida's divers keep coming back. In 2026 they pulled out a record 36,187. No one has shown that this has pushed any Florida reef below that line. But a job with no end date needs people who want to do it. Carlson still loves hunting them. And his freezer is full.
