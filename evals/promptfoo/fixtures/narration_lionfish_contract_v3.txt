Paul Carlson’s freezer is full of lionfish: the striped invader he hunts, removes, and prefers on a plate to hogfish, grouper, or lobster. Carlson earned the 2025 recreational Lionfish King title by removing 1,542 of them over 73 dive trips. His freezer is a trophy case, but also a management question. In an ocean where lionfish encounter few predators, can Florida make people hungry enough to become the predator instead? Carlson’s answer begins with a contradiction he seems willing to live with: he says he loves killing lionfish, even though he calls the fish beautiful, and he loves hunting them and getting them. The same animal that fills his freezer is admired, pursued, and eaten because it is damaging waters where it does not belong. To understand why a beautiful aquarium fish became a target, the story has to go back to Florida’s Atlantic coast. Lionfish were first reported off Florida’s Atlantic Coast near Dania Beach in 1985. The most probable explanation for their arrival in the Atlantic is the aquarium trade, and experts have speculated that unwanted lionfish were dumped from home aquariums into the ocean. That matters because the famous story about a large aquarium breaking during Hurricane Andrew is probably erroneous, according to the supplied record. So the invasion is not best understood as a freak storm releasing a monster, but as a human-assisted introduction that then escaped human control. By 2002, lionfishes had been established from Miami to North Carolina; by December 2009, the first confirmed Gulf specimens had been taken; and by 2010, lionfishes were established from Mexico through Venezuela. Larval dispersal in ocean currents helped carry them across the Caribbean region. A Florida sighting had become a basin-wide invasion, which means no single cleanup could simply rewind the story. The next problem was that Atlantic ecosystems did not seem to have enough natural control to absorb the newcomer. Other than cannibalism, there are few documented natural predators of lionfish. In their native range, lionfish populations are controlled by predation, competition, and disease, but in the Atlantic, Gulf of Mexico, and Caribbean they encounter few predators and can reproduce year-round in warm waters. NOAA has also framed the problem starkly, saying lionfish have no known predators and reproduce all year long. That is the title’s problem in practical form: not that every possible predator is absent everywhere, but that the usual checks are missing or weak where the invasion is established. When those checks are weak, one lionfish can matter. In a controlled five-week experiment, a single lionfish reduced net recruitment of juvenile native fishes by an average of 79 percent compared with reefs with no lionfish. In one study, lionfish abundance increased rapidly between 2004 and 2010, until lionfish comprised nearly 40 percent of total predator biomass in the system. That increase coincided with a 65 percent decline in the biomass of 42 Atlantic prey fishes in just two years. The pressure can reach into the reef system itself, because lionfish also have the potential to decrease parrotfish and other herbivorous fishes that keep seaweeds and macroalgae from overgrowing corals. Those documented effects are why Carlson’s freezer works as a symbol, while the research shows what sustained control can protect: juvenile recruitment, prey biomass, and reef functions managers are trying to defend. If nature is not applying enough pressure, managers have tried to recruit people to apply it instead. NOAA developed an “Eat Lionfish” campaign that brought together fishing communities, wholesalers, and chefs to broaden U.S. consumers’ awareness of the invader. NOAA scientists have concurred that developing a market for lionfish is one of the only ways to substantially reduce their numbers. Florida also lowered barriers for recreational harvest: a recreational fishing license is not required for recreational fishers targeting lionfish with specified gear such as a pole spear, Hawaiian Sling, handheld net, or lionfish-specific spearing device. That policy fits the biology of the work, because lionfish are rarely caught on hook and line, and the most effective removal methods are spearing and using a handheld net. The idea is not just to warn people about an invader, but to give divers a reason to do slow, repeated removal work. Carlson’s 73 dive trips show what that looks like when one recreational hunter keeps coming back. And he was not alone. In the FWC’s 2025 Lionfish Challenge, more than 500 divers made 1,104 dive trips and removed more than 30,000 invasive lionfish during the summer-long tournament. In the 2026 Lionfish Challenge, 533 registered participants removed a record 36,187 lionfish over 1,468 dive trips. Those numbers make the strategy real rather than symbolic: hundreds of divers can turn competition, status, and the promise of food into tens of thousands of removals. But big totals create the harder question, because lionfish are built to replace losses quickly and hide where people are harder to send. Female lionfish release two gelatinous egg masses of about 12,000 to 15,000 eggs each, and in warmer climates lionfish can spawn every four days. NOAA says a mature female releases roughly two million eggs a year. Lionfish inhabit natural reef and artificial structure from just a few inches of water to more than 300 meters deep, and Florida’s wildlife agency says they have been found in waters up to 1,000 feet deep. They can also occur in almost all estuarine and marine habitat types, which makes the invasion much larger than a line of easy dive sites. Scientists have therefore narrowed the promise: complete eradication of lionfish in the Atlantic is likely impossible, but focused control efforts in strategic locations are prudent. NOAA researchers have similarly concluded that invasive lionfish populations cannot be eliminated using conventional methods, and that marine invaders are nearly impossible to eradicate once established. Still, control can matter where it is sustained. Researchers monitored 24 Bahamian patch reefs over 18 months to study the consequences of lionfish removal for native fish populations. On reefs where lionfish were kept below threshold densities, native prey fish biomass increased by 50 to 70 percent. Partial removals produced similar gains in prey fish biomass to full removals, while taking 30 percent less time to carry out. That changes the goal from wiping out lionfish everywhere to suppressing them in the places where removal can give native fish room to recover. But even that practical goal has practical limits. A UF researcher noted that finding high lionfish densities generally requires fishers to dive fairly deep, often past 130 feet, which requires trained and experienced divers. A UF study found that, under current economic conditions, profit margins for commercially harvested lionfish are relatively low, which may explain why many commercial fishers are not targeting the species. A bioeconomic model in a case study of invasive lionfish on artificial reefs in the northeastern Gulf of Mexico projected relatively small potential profits in the fishery. And there is a paradox inside any successful fishery for an invader: reducing the population reduces catch rates and can make harvest uneconomical. In other words, the better people do at clearing lionfish from a place, the harder it may become to keep clearing that place for money. That is why Carlson’s role is larger than one freezer and smaller than a cure. His dives belong to a culture of repeated recreational removal that Florida can count, reward, and encourage, even when commercial harvest alone is fragile. So yes: NOAA has promoted a market for eating lionfish, while Florida has made recreational removal easier, and together that broader strategy can support control, but not extinction. NOAA researchers say established marine invaders are nearly impossible to eradicate, and lionfish can live deep and reproduce year-round. The strategy is to create demand that gives people a reason to keep removing them. In the right places, those removals can help native fish recover. That is why Carlson’s full freezer matters: in the Atlantic, people have become one of the few practical predators available.
