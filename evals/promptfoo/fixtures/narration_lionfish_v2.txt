You want dinner. Florida would like you to eat an invasion.

Paul Carlson can recommend the fish. In the summer of 2025, he made seventy-three dive trips and removed more than fifteen hundred lionfish.

His report afterward? "My freezer is full."

The plan sounds delicious. But for people catching lionfish for a living, there's a strange problem:

What happens when you get good at removing the thing you're paid to catch?

You can see why people wanted lionfish in aquariums. Stripes. Flowing fins. A fish dressed like it's expecting photographers.

The trouble began when they turned up outside the glass.

Lionfish are native to the Indo-Pacific. Aquarium releases are the leading explanation for their arrival in Florida's waters. Here, they found small fish to eat and very few predators to keep their numbers down.

And those small fish matter. Some graze on algae that can overgrow coral. Others become food for larger fish. A reef needs its next generation to survive long enough to do those jobs.

In one five-week experiment on small reefs in the Bahamas, researchers counted nearly eighty percent fewer new young native fish on reefs with a single lionfish than on reefs without one.

One fish. A very busy mouth.

So scientists encouraged people to put that mouth on the menu.

NOAA promoted eating lionfish. Florida encouraged people to remove them. The idea was appealing: divers catch the invader, chefs turn it into dinner, and customers help pay for the next trip.

Government advice you could serve with lemon.

And for a diver, there is something satisfying about such a clear task.

Picture yourself underwater. A lionfish hovers against the reef. You get close, line up your spear, and make the catch. Carefully: those spines carry venom.

Into the collecting container. On to the next fish.

You can see your progress. Every fish you bring up is one fewer predator feeding on that reef.

Across Florida, those individual catches add up. The state's 2026 Lionfish Challenge removed more than thirty-six thousand fish.

The competition has a closing date. The fish keep breeding.

In warm water, female lionfish can spawn about every four days. Their young can travel on currents. The reef you've worked on is still connected to the rest of the sea.

And the fish aren't all waiting at convenient diving depths. Lionfish live hundreds of feet down, beyond the reach of ordinary recreational dives.

Your appetite can be enormous. Your time underwater cannot.

That is what sits behind a number like Paul's seventy-three trips. Find the fish. Bring them up. Go out again.

Paul enjoyed hunting lionfish. He was competing recreationally. But now put yourself on a commercial boat, trying to earn a living from the catch.

Fuel costs money. Equipment costs money. So does the time you spend searching instead of catching.

Then suppose the removals work. There are fewer lionfish on the reef.

Good news—until you try to fill the next order.

You search longer. You bring back fewer fish. The boat burns fuel just the same.

You've made progress on the reef, and the next trip can become less worth making.

University of Florida researchers saw this tradeoff in a model of a Gulf of Mexico lionfish fishery, where potential profits were already slim.

A restaurant needs a steady supply. Conservation means trying to leave fewer lionfish in the water. Eventually, the remaining fish can cost more to catch than they bring in.

The plan can run out of profit long before the ocean runs out of lionfish.

So what do you do? Keep paying people to search for the very last one?

On some reefs, that may be more work than protecting the native fish requires.

Researchers tested removals on twenty-four small reefs in the Bahamas. Where they kept lionfish below levels expected to harm the local fish community, the total weight of native fish increased.

The surprising part: removing some lionfish produced benefits similar to removing them all from those experimental reefs—and took less time.

The native fish could benefit before the last lionfish was gone.

That gives the effort a more practical target. Work out how much removal a particular reef needs. Then work out how to keep that effort going.

It won't work equally well everywhere. You have to check how the native fish are doing. A big catch alone doesn't prove a reef is recovering.

And dinner won't pay for every trip. Competitions give recreational divers another reason to join in. Paul won Florida's Lionfish King title for his summer's work.

He also got something easier to appreciate than a conservation statistic.

He told a reporter he'd choose lionfish over lobster.

A hunt he enjoyed. A title. A freezer full of dinners he actually wanted to eat.

So look again at that full freezer.

It shows what came out of the water.

The bigger prize is what those trips can leave on a reef: young native fish with a chance to grow up.

That's the part of a successful fishing trip you leave behind.
