You'd think the ocean would sort out an invader on its own. It didn't. A single lionfish cut the young fish settling on one reef by 79% in five weeks. Ecologist Mark Hixon called them voracious, with no natural controls. So why is Florida asking people to eat them? So the agencies turned to the one predator the ocean had left: people. NOAA built an 'Eat Lionfish' campaign — fishers, wholesalers, chefs — and you need no license to spear them. The flesh is safe to eat. Put the invader on the plate. So for a while, it looked like it was working. Across Florida in a single year, organized hunts and derbies pulled nearly 100,000 lionfish out of the water. Spear by spear, plate by plate, people were eating their way through the invasion. But the math says they cannot be eliminated. One study suggested about 27 percent of mature lionfish would have to be removed every month, for a year, just to stop the population growing. Not to shrink it. Just to hold it. Here is what breaks it. A hard cull can clear lionfish from a reef, but the moment the spearing slows, the empty reef fills back in. And in warm water, a lionfish can spawn every four days. Still, the reef was paying the whole time. Hixon reported that researchers had to abandon some reef fish studies because lionfish had moved in and were eating everything. By 2010 they made up nearly 40% of predator weight on one system, as their prey collapsed by 65% in two years. But every reef has a floor few hunters can reach. Lionfish live on shallow coral, yes — but also on deep ridges, far below where a diver with a spear can safely work. Most of the hunting happens in the easy water, close to the surface. That is where the rules allow it, where the air lasts, where a person can work a reef without special training. The invaders below just wait. So where are the fish thickest? Down past a hundred and thirty feet — a depth that demands trained, experienced divers. The densest colonies sit exactly where the fewest people can go. That is the first wall. But there is a second wall, and it has nothing to do with depth. A University of Florida study ran the numbers on hauling lionfish up for market — and found the profit margins are thin. Every trip burns fuel, time, and labor. When the reward for a hard day barely clears the cost, the fish you spear for dinner is not the fish you chase for a living. The economics push commercial boats elsewhere, and the species keeps slipping past the market. And there is a third wall waiting on the plate itself. Federal regulators cooled on the eat-them push after testing nearly two hundred lionfish turned up something uncomfortable: more than a quarter carried too much of a toxin that can cause ciguatera. But here is the twist that made people push back. When the report was written, not one confirmed ciguatera case in the whole country had ever been traced to a lionfish. The warning stood on a threshold, not on a single sick diner. Then, in the warm Gulf, something the hunters never arranged began to happen. In August of 2017, lionfish started turning up with open sores across their skin — a raw ulcerative disease spreading through the population. At its peak, up to forty percent of them carried it — a sickness no diver had unleashed. Then the count on the reefs fell. On natural reefs the next year, surveys found three in every four lionfish simply gone — a collapse no spear, no derby, no campaign had managed. But people did not stop hunting. In Florida's 2026 summer-long tournament, 533 registered participants pulled a record thirty-six thousand lionfish from the water — over five thousand more fish than they had speared the year before. The summer before had already set a mark: more than eleven hundred dive trips. Every season the number climbs. The agency that runs the tournament keeps saying the same thing. Year after year, its marine fisheries director tells reporters they are blown away by how many people turn up to dive, to spear, to keep hunting a fish nothing else will. So why does Florida keep asking people to eat lionfish? Because nothing else in the Atlantic will, and the fish breeds faster than any single hunt can clear. That is the strange bargain Florida made. It did not win the reef back; it hired a season that never ends, a hunt that has to be won again every summer just to hold the line. So look again at that one red-and-white fish on the spear. It is not a trophy, and it is not a solution. It is one fish from an ocean still making more — and the only hand reaching for the next one is ours.
