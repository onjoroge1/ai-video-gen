# Why Hanoi's Rat Bounty Created Rat Farms

Target: 300 seconds. Engine candidate: `backfiring_solution`. Status: untested synthetic editorial reference. Thumbnail concept: one tail beside a coin, with a living rat behind the counting desk. No graphic injury. All narration is original; historical claims are attributed below.

## Narration

<!-- NARRATION_START -->
### B01

Hanoi pays people to get rid of rats. Then officials discover people raising them. The animals the city wants gone have become something worth keeping alive. How does a rat-killing campaign end up rewarding rat farms? Start with the little piece of evidence handed over for payment. Not the whole animal. Just its tail. That detail will pull the plan apart.

### B02

It is 1902. Hanoi is under French colonial rule, and its new sewers are supposed to represent a cleaner, more modern city. But the underground network also gives rats somewhere to live and move. Historian Michael Vann describes reports of rats reaching French homes through their plumbing. A project meant to separate people from waste creates another route between people and rats.

### B03

This is more than an unpleasant surprise in a bathroom. The third plague pandemic has made rats a major concern for public-health authorities. Cities far beyond Hanoi are studying them, trapping them and trying to keep them away from people. The danger is serious. But an urgent reason to act does not tell the city how to measure whether its action works.

### B04

Vietnamese rat catchers go into the sewers. The administration records their daily kills. Much later, Vann finds those records in the archives: sheets of totals that climb into the thousands, with one day exceeding twenty thousand. Imagine that column filling a screen. It shows work being recorded at an enormous scale. What it cannot show on its own is how many rats remain.

### B05

The authorities also offer residents a bounty. Bring evidence of a rat killed, receive money. Bringing thousands of carcasses into the city centre would create its own problem, so tails become the evidence. It is a compact, countable substitute. And the deliveries look encouraging. To the official counting them, every additional tail appears to mean one fewer rat outside. But appearance is doing important work here.

### B06

A tail proves that someone obtained a tail. It does not prove that the rat is dead. Vann's account describes living rats without tails and the discovery of rat farms outside Hanoi. No imaginary mastermind is needed to explain the mismatch. The payment rule asks for one thing; the public-health objective requires another. Someone can satisfy the first while actively undermining the second.

### B07

Now the business changes. Hunting depends on finding an animal that already exists. Farming creates a supply. A rat kept for breeding can produce offspring whose tails qualify for payment. That is the economic reversal, not a claim that every resident becomes a breeder. An animal previously treated as a nuisance acquires value because the authorities have created a buyer for part of it.

### B08

The response does not stop at local farms. Vann also describes a trade bringing rats from elsewhere in northern Vietnam to collect Hanoi's reward. The scheme now reaches beyond the place it was supposed to protect. More deliveries can come from a larger supply network, rather than a shrinking local population. The collection desk sees another transaction. The city needs to see where that transaction began.

### B09

There is a reason to be precise about what this history proves. The evidence supports farming, trafficking and a payment rule people exploited. It does not give us permission to invent a final rat census, a multiplication rate, or a death toll caused by the bounty. Nor were Vietnamese residents background characters in a French success story. Their choices expose how limited the administration's control actually was.

### B10

Return to the desk. The coin changes hands. A tail lands in the pile. On paper, another unit of success. Outside that frame, a rat can still be alive, and somewhere else another can be bred. The failure was not that people ignored the reward. They responded to it. Hanoi wanted fewer rats. What it bought was tails. Those were never the same thing.
