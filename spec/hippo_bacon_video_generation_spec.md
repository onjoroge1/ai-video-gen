# Video Generation Specification

## America Almost Ate Hippo Meat — The America We Avoided

**Status:** Production-ready Claude handoff  
**Prepared:** August 28, 2026  
**Format:** Evidence Mystery / historical counterfactual documentary  
**Target runtime:** 8:25–8:45  
**Target narration:** 1,270–1,330 words after final measured timing  
**Aspect ratio:** 16:9, 3840×2160 master, 1920×1080 minimum  
**Audience:** Curious adults 18–44 who watch history, animals, food science, and “strange but true” explainers  
**Primary goal:** High first-minute retention with a complete story, not an expanded listicle

## 1. Executive production decision

### Primary title

**America Almost Ate Hippo Meat. Here’s What Stopped It**

### Thumbnail promise

Large retail package labeled **LAKE COW BACON** in a modern meat case, a hippo eye and snout looming behind the glass, and two words only:

**HIPPO BACON?**

The package, hippo, and text are the only three focal elements. Do not add a politician, map, arrow, extra animal, or Bolt to the thumbnail.

### Logline

In 1910, Louisiana congressman Robert F. Broussard tried to solve America’s meat shortage and the South’s invasive-plant crisis with one audacious import: African hippos. We follow his proposal toward a congressional decision, then run the world it promised against modern zoology and Colombia’s accidental hippo experiment.

### Core thesis

This is not primarily a story about why humans never domesticated hippos. It is a story about a seductive policy idea that appeared to solve two emergencies at once—and about the ecological disaster America may have avoided when a committee refused to advance it.

### Creative rule

Borrow the **validated audience question**, not the source video’s wording, sequence, thesis, examples, or visual treatment. The finished work must be independently written, independently researched, and visibly different.

## 2. What the source video proves

Source benchmark: [Why We Don’t Eat Hippo meat?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HRDwJ_BhaLk), Bright Psycho.

At review time, YouTube showed approximately **436,000 views after four days**, **3,700 likes**, **573 comments**, a **16:41 runtime**, and a channel size of approximately **12,100 subscribers**. That is roughly 36 views for every subscriber—a powerful outlier signal. The user’s earlier observation of 434,855 views is consistent with the live count continuing to rise.

### Why the idea traveled

1. **Immediate category violation:** A hippo in a grocery meat case feels wrong before the viewer knows why.
2. **Simple title question:** The packaging is understood in under one second.
3. **Absurd-but-true history:** A serious U.S. proposal gives the premise credibility and escalation.
4. **Personal relevance:** The story begins with something the viewer sees every week—the meat aisle.
5. **Expandable payoff:** One animal can lead into policy, ecology, agriculture, danger, and historical path dependence.
6. **Comment fuel:** Viewers can debate whether hippos are edible, farmable, dangerous, or culturally unfamiliar.

### Weaknesses our version must exploit

1. The source says the bill passed committee and came within a few votes. The stronger historical account says the committee remained unconvinced and shelved it. Do **not** repeat the near-passage claim.
2. The source suggests hippos would consume aquatic plants in enormous quantities. Modern zoological evidence says they mainly leave the water at night to graze grass; aquatic plants are a small dietary component.
3. Its central answer arrives late, then becomes a long six-test domestication list. The story protagonist disappears for much of the middle.
4. The conclusion repeats the same inherited-food-system idea several times.
5. Visible audience comments flag factual imprecision, excessive verbal padding, and malformed AI graphics.
6. The title has more reach than the execution. That is good news: there is room for a more accurate, more cinematic version.

**Production conclusion:** The demand signal is real. The correct response is a tighter narrative with better evidence, not a paraphrase.

## 3. The novel angle: “The America We Avoided”

The film begins in an alternate 2026 where Broussard’s idea succeeded. A shopper sees vacuum-sealed “Lake Cow Bacon.” A highway sign warns of hippos crossing. A local news screen reports another escaped animal in Louisiana.

At 0:18, the film reveals that this world is counterfactual—but the bill was real.

The rest of the video answers one forward-driving question:

> **What would have happened if Congress had said yes?**

The narrative first lets Broussard’s idea feel ingenious. Only after viewers understand why intelligent people supported it do we reveal the biological flaw. Colombia then acts as real-world evidence, transforming the counterfactual from speculation into an informed warning.

This structure creates four consecutive viewer beliefs:

| Stage | Viewer belief |
|---|---|
| Opening | “This alternate supermarket is ridiculous.” |
| Historical reveal | “Wait—the United States seriously considered this.” |
| Seduction | “For 1910, this may actually have sounded clever.” |
| Reversal | “The animal would not solve the plant problem and could become the new invasive problem.” |
| Final belief | “A boring committee failure may have prevented an ecological nightmare.” |

7. **Viewer reinterprets the ending:** The bill’s failure is not anticlimax; it is the consequence.

## 7. Locked narration draft

Claude may polish individual sentences for measured timing and spoken naturalness, but it must not change the plot, facts, reveal order, or ending without flagging the change. Final narration must remain within 1,270–1,330 words and 8:25–8:45 at the selected voice’s measured—not estimated—pace.

### 0:00–0:45 — The impossible grocery aisle

Imagine walking into a Louisiana supermarket in 2026. Beside the beef is a blue package labeled “Lake Cow Bacon.” Outside, a yellow sign warns drivers about hippos crossing. The local news is tracking another two-ton animal loose near a school.

This America never existed. But in 1910, a Louisiana congressman asked Washington to build it.

His proposal promised to solve two emergencies with one animal: cheap meat for crowded American cities and open waterways for the South. The animal was the hippopotamus. The bill was real. The hearing was serious. And today, another country has accidentally run part of the experiment for us.

### 0:45–1:32 — Two problems looking for one answer

At the beginning of the twentieth century, American families were angry about the price of meat. Cattle supplies, feed costs, and powerful meatpackers all became targets as beef grew harder for working families to afford.

Louisiana had a different emergency. Water hyacinth—an ornamental plant imported from South America—had escaped into Southern waterways. It spread into thick floating mats, slowed boats, interfered with fishing, and resisted efforts to remove or burn it.

Representative Robert F. Broussard looked at expensive meat, a plant nobody wanted, and thousands of acres of warm wet habitat. To him, the crises appeared to fit together like two pieces of a machine.

### 1:32–2:35 — Broussard sells the dream

In March 1910, Broussard brought House Resolution 23261 before the House Committee on Agriculture. It sought 250,000 dollars—about eight million in 2023 purchasing power—to import useful wild animals into the United States.

Hippos would be the star. Release them into Louisiana and Florida. Let them consume the unwanted vegetation. When they grew large, turn them into an affordable new meat supply.

Supporters gave the product a friendlier name: lake cow bacon.

Broussard arrived with men whose biographies made the scheme feel adventurous rather than absurd: American scout Frederick Russell Burnham, South African soldier Fritz Duquesne, and Agriculture Department researcher William Newton Irwin. They described hippo meat as rich, usable food and argued that the animals could thrive on waterfront farms.

Even former president Theodore Roosevelt supported the broader idea. For a country anxious about food, the pitch had a seductive shape: no sacrifice, no rationing—just turn one invasion into dinner.

### 2:35–3:25 — The plan becomes a new America

Then the proposal grew larger.

Irwin told the committee that imported animals could add as much as one million tons of meat to the national supply. Hippos were only the opening act. Antelope might live on family farms. African buffalo could range across the West. Yaks could occupy the Rockies. Rhinoceroses were imagined in the Southwest.

It was an agricultural version of redesigning a continent: move animals to whatever American landscape resembled their old home, then harvest the result.

Newspapers debated the idea. Supporters treated public disgust as a failure of imagination. Americans already ate cattle that had once been foreign imports. Why should a hippo be different?

The answer depended on two questions the committee was about to ask.

### 3:25–4:20 — The two questions

First: could hippos be contained on ordinary farms?

Second: would they actually eat the water hyacinth?

Broussard’s side answered yes to both. They pictured manageable animals living behind fences on small waterfront properties, feeding on dense mats of free vegetation.

From inside the hearing room, those answers completed the machine. Plant becomes hippo. Hippo becomes meat. Two public problems disappear.

But the machine was built around an animal they did not understand and a plant with almost no nutritional value. Modern evidence changes both answers.

Hippos spend their days in water, but that does not mean water plants are their normal fuel. At night they climb onto land and graze—often traveling miles for grass. Aquatic plants make up only a small portion of their diet.

And water hyacinth is roughly 95 percent water. An animal trying to live on it would process enormous bulk for very little energy.

### 4:20–5:25 — The solution becomes the new problem

So picture the first Louisiana herd after the novelty fades.

The hippos do not remain politely inside a carpet of hyacinth. They leave the water after dark searching for grass. They move along banks, through crops, across roads, and toward new waterways.

Now add containment. Adult hippos are territorial, immensely powerful, and dangerous around boats and shorelines. A fence that holds cattle is not proof against a determined animal weighing several thousand pounds. Every escape turns a farm problem into a public one.

Their waste also moves nutrients from land into water. In an African ecosystem, that flow is part of a long-established food web. In a foreign river, concentrated nutrients can change oxygen levels, algae, fish, and water quality.

The original promise was that hippos would repair an invaded ecosystem. The more plausible result is that America would add a mobile megaherbivore to the invasive plant it already could not control.

That is an informed counterfactual. But it is not merely imagination, because a version of it began in Colombia.

### 5:25–6:55 — The accidental experiment

In the 1980s, drug trafficker Pablo Escobar imported four hippos for a private zoo at Hacienda Nápoles. After his death, most exotic animals were moved. The hippos were difficult to capture, so they remained.

They bred. Some left the estate. Their descendants spread through the Magdalena River basin.

By 2026, the population was estimated at around two hundred animals. Scientists have documented rapid population growth and warned about altered water chemistry, habitat disturbance, conflict with people, and pressure on native species. Colombia has tried sterilization, relocation, confinement, and euthanasia planning—each expensive, difficult, or politically painful.

Four founders did not need to be domesticated. They only needed warm water, grass, room to spread, and time.

Now move that lesson north. Broussard was not proposing four animals behind a private fence. His vision required a food industry: breeding stock, waterfront farms, transport routes, workers, slaughter facilities, and repeated contact between people and hippos. It also imagined releases across landscapes already struggling with an invasive species.

Colombia cannot tell us exactly what 1910 Louisiana would have become. It can tell us that “we will control them later” is not a plan.

### 6:55–7:45 — What really happened in Congress

Back in Washington, the Agriculture Committee did not send America rushing toward hippo ranches. Members remained unconvinced, and the proposal was shelved.

There was no dramatic national vote that defeated hippo bacon by a handful of ballots. That version makes a better legend, but the documented ending is quieter: the plan failed to advance.

Broussard considered trying again. Then his political ambitions shifted toward the Senate, World War I transformed American priorities, and meat production changed. The hippo idea slipped from public policy into historical trivia.

That quiet ending is exactly why the story matters. Catastrophe does not always end with a heroic rescue. Sometimes it ends with skepticism in a committee room, an unanswered practical question, and a folder placed on a shelf.

### 7:45–8:35 — The shelf that never existed

So return to that alternate grocery store.

The lake cow bacon looks like proof that Broussard succeeded. But follow the package backward and the rest of the world comes with it: fenced bayous, dangerous night grazing, altered rivers, costly control programs, and communities forced to live with a decision made generations earlier.

Hippo meat itself was never the impossible part. People have eaten it. Witnesses in 1910 said it tasted somewhere between familiar meats. The impossible promise was that America could import a powerful animal, aim it at an ecological problem, and receive only the benefit.

The package disappears. The warning sign disappears. The grocery case returns to the one we know.

History is usually explained by what people built—the laws they passed, the machines they invented, the animals they domesticated. But our world is also shaped by plans that stopped before they became normal.

The strangest thing in this meat aisle is not what is here.

It is the product a skeptical committee kept off the shelf.

## 8. Visual system

### Overall look

**Cinematic historical documentary with explicit evidence layers.** Photorealistic environments, tactile archival textures, restrained typography, and clear labeling of hypothetical imagery. Avoid the generic montage of unrelated AI images common to automated explainer channels.

### Three visual worlds

| World | Palette | Camera language | On-screen label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alternate 2026 | Clean supermarket cyan, sodium-yellow roads, ominous red alerts | Smooth dolly, shallow depth of field, precise product macro | **COUNTERFACTUAL — 2026** |
| Historical 1910 | Tobacco brown, faded navy, warm window light, black ink | Slow handheld restraint, rostrum moves over real documents, period reenactment inserts | **DRAMATIZATION** or exact archive credit |
| Modern evidence | Natural greens and muddy river blues | Documentary telephoto, satellite/map animation, measured graphics | **COLOMBIA — MODERN EVIDENCE** |

### Recurring object continuity

The “Lake Cow Bacon” package must remain identical every time:

- Matte blue paper wrap
- Cream oval label
- Small black hippo silhouette
- Red circular seal reading “EST. 1910”
- Product name “LAKE COW BACON”
- No fake USDA seal
- No invented nutrition or health claims

Use one approved reference frame for all subsequent appearances. Do not regenerate the package independently in each shot.

### Broussard continuity

Use the public-domain House/Library of Congress portrait as the authoritative facial reference. Create one locked character sheet before generating reenactments. Wardrobe: dark 1910 frock coat, high white collar, conservative tie. Never change age, hairline, facial hair, or body build across shots. If reference consistency cannot be achieved, use silhouette, hands, over-shoulder framing, and the real portrait rather than an inconsistent face.

### Evidence versus dramatization

- Every real historical document must be sourced and credited in the artifact manifest.
- Do not fabricate a newspaper headline and present it as archival.
- Reenactments must carry a subtle **DRAMATIZATION** label on first appearance and after any long break.
- Counterfactual Louisiana footage must carry **COUNTERFACTUAL** on first appearance and during the simulated timeline.
- Colombia footage may use licensed/current stock, properly sourced stills, maps, or clearly labeled generated reconstruction. Do not depict a generated scene as news footage.

### Bolt usage

Bolt is an evidence guide, not the protagonist.

1. 0:36–0:41 — Bolt places an evidence pin on **H.R. 23261 / 1910**.
2. 4:02–4:08 — Bolt rotates the bright “promise” diagram to reveal the red “biology” side.
3. 8:18–8:24 — Bolt silently removes the counterfactual package as the real shelf returns.

Total Bolt screen time: 12–18 seconds, under 4 percent of runtime. Every appearance must perform a useful story action. No idle reaction shots, lip-sync, or repeated pointing.

### Motion priorities

Reserve full-motion generation for moments where action carries meaning:

1. Grocery dolly reveal of hippo bacon
2. Hippo crossing behind a Louisiana road sign
3. Water hyacinth closing over a boat channel
4. Bill landing on the committee desk
5. Proposal map populating with imported species
6. Hippo leaving water at night to graze
7. Fence failure / escape, shown without gore
8. Counterfactual Louisiana-to-Colombia match cut
9. Colombian herd moving through river habitat
10. Committee room emptying as the bill remains behind
11. Final package removal and shelf transformation

All other coverage may use evidence stills, restrained 2.5D parallax, map animation, macro inserts, document highlights, and continuity-preserving reframes.

In 1910, a Louisiana congressman asked the United States to import African hippos, release them into Southern wetlands, and turn them into a new source of affordable meat—“lake cow bacon.” The plan was supposed to solve two emergencies at once: rising meat prices and waterways choked by invasive water hyacinth.

### Prohibited claims

- “The bill passed committee.”
- “America came within a few votes of hippo farming.”
- “Hippos are slow in water.”
- “Hippos eat aquatic vegetation in enormous quantities.”
