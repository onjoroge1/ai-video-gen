## What Happens If You Fire a Bullet on Every Planet?

**Document status:** Production master  
**Version:** 2.0  
**Format:** YouTube Short  
**Target runtime:** 39–43 seconds  
**Canvas:** 1080 × 1920, 9:16 vertical  
**Frame rate:** 30 fps master

**Primary KPI:** Reproduce the control video’s repeated-payoff and replay behavior while improving first-frame acceptance.

---

## 1. Executive production directive

This is **not** a sequence of unrelated AI illustrations and **not** a Bolt-led firearms montage.

It is one controlled experiment:

> **The same projectile is launched at the same speed, from the same launcher, at the same near-horizontal angle, while only the planet’s gravity and atmosphere change.**

The bullet is the protagonist. Its path is the story.

Every shot must preserve the same visual grammar so the viewer can compare outcomes instantly. Bolt appears only where he adds narrative value: the hook, the experiment setup, and the Pluto payoff.

The production must be built from:

1. Generated planetary environment plates
2. One locked launcher asset
3. One locked projectile asset
4. Deterministic projectile and trajectory animation
5. Minimal Bolt overlays
6. Captions, labels, sound and transitions added in post

The image or video generator must **not** be responsible for inventing the projectile’s motion.

---

# 2. Creative thesis

The viewer should feel they are watching the same test repeated under progressively stranger conditions.

The retention engine is:

> **Immediate question → visible result → new planet → more extreme result → Pluto reversal**

Each planet must provide a visual payoff within approximately three seconds. The video should escalate from familiar ballistic motion to atmospheric extremes, then to worlds with no solid surface, and finally to a theoretical Pluto orbit that sends the bullet back toward the launch point.

---

# 3. Core premise contract

## Viewer question

**What happens when the exact same bullet is fired under the conditions of every planet?**

## Promised answer

- Earth establishes the baseline.
- Venus stops the bullet fastest because of atmospheric drag.
- Mars lets it travel much farther.
- Mercury wins among the rocky planets.
- The giant planets remove the idea of “landing.”
- Pluto provides the bonus twist: under narrowly defined conditions, the bullet could theoretically enter a low orbit and circle back toward where it started.

## Final takeaway

> **Same projectile. Different world. Gravity and atmosphere decide the path.**

# 5. Safety and presentation boundaries

This is an educational physics simulation.

- Bolt never handles a realistic firearm.
- Use a fictional, unbranded robotic test launcher.
- Do not show ammunition loading, weapon operation, aiming technique or procedural firing instructions.
- Do not show a human target.
- The Pluto projectile approaches Bolt as a stylized non-impact danger cue; cut or loop before contact.
- Keep the visual focus on planetary physics, not weapon mechanics.

# 7. Science communication rules

## Allowed claims

- Atmosphere creates drag.
- Gravity curves the projectile downward.
- Venus’s dense atmosphere causes rapid deceleration.
- Mars’s thin atmosphere and weaker gravity allow a longer trajectory than Earth.
- Mercury has weak gravity and almost no atmosphere, producing the longest rocky-world trajectory in this standardized comparison.
- Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune do not offer a conventional solid surface for the bullet to strike.
- On Pluto, a sufficiently fast near-horizontal projectile could theoretically approach orbital motion.

## Required qualifications

The Pluto sequence must use one of these phrases:

- **“could theoretically”**
- **“at the right speed, altitude and angle”**
- **“in an idealized simulation”**

Never state that an ordinary shot from any location on Pluto will automatically circle the world and hit the shooter.

## Claims to avoid

- Exact range numbers unless separately modeled and validated
- “No gravity” on any world
- “No atmosphere” when “almost no atmosphere” is more accurate
- A projectile reaching a solid surface on a giant planet
- Sound traveling normally through a near-vacuum
- A candle burning in a vacuum
- Flowers, feathers, anvils, pendulums or gauges as substitutes for visible projectile behavior

# 19. Hard rejection gates

A render is rejected automatically when any of the following occurs.

## Premise failures

- The first frame does not show the return-path concept.
- The same-shot rule is not stated by 0:04.5.
- Pluto is presented as one of the eight planets.
- The final takeaway is missing.

## Continuity failures

- Projectile design changes.
- Launcher design changes.
- Launch angle changes.
- Projectile direction reverses without the intentional Pluto orbit.
- Camera changes during Earth, Venus, Mars or Mercury.
- Bullet scale changes by more than 10% in comparison scenes.
- Bolt develops legs, feet or boots.
- Bolt holds a realistic rifle.

## Visual-logic failures

- Venus does not visibly decelerate fastest.
- Mars does not visibly exceed Earth’s trajectory.
- Mercury is not the clear rocky-world winner.
- A gas or ice giant is shown with a solid impact surface.
- Neptune’s wind makes the projectile appear powered or self-steering.
- Pluto trajectory teleports, forms an impossible corner or strikes terrain before completing the visual loop.

## Creative failures

- Symbolic props appear.
- Bolt becomes the dominant subject in more than three scenes.
- More than 40% of scenes use generic starfield composition instead of the actual local environment.
- Planet sections feel like unrelated posters.
- The bullet is not the clearest moving object.
- The ending becomes a static outro.

## Pacing failures

- Any ordinary planet scene exceeds 3.5 seconds.
- Earth exceeds 3 seconds.
- The Pluto setup exceeds 3 seconds before orbital motion begins.
- A caption remains after its visual payoff has already passed.
