Bolt Explains the World: one sourced intervention backfire (an animal moved, an invention adopted, or a rule enforced that did the opposite of its purpose), ~5-minute landscape illustrated documentary.

OPENING: open inside one person's practical problem, in the second person -- imagine you run fish ponds or a wastewater lagoon in the American South (use whichever setting the sources document), and the water keeps turning green with algae and plankton that you would rather not fight with chemicals. Make the need tangible before any date or name. Give the decision (bringing in plankton-eating carp from Asia to graze the water clean) its rationale as the sources give it. Reach the first unintended consequence -- fish getting out of the ponds and into the river system, by whatever route the sources document -- as efficiently as the story allows. The body begins after that consequence and never re-tells the problem, the decision or the escape.

Differ from what is already on YouTube: the top videos cover the fight against the fish, the jumping-fish spectacle, and harvesting them out of the Illinois River. This film tells the backfire: why someone wanted these fish, how the tool for cleaner water became the problem, and what the fish do to the river from inside the food web. Keep the jumping-fish scene to one moment, not the spine.

Causal arc (as questions for research to answer, not facts): Which carp species were imported (silver, bighead, grass, black), by whom, when, and for what stated purpose -- aquaculture, algae control, sewage lagoon treatment, research? How did they escape into open water, and what do the sources say about floods or other routes? How did they spread up the Mississippi and its tributaries, in what order? What do they eat, and which native animals depend on the same plankton? What happens to boaters when silver carp are disturbed? What has been built or tried to stop them (barriers, commercial harvest, renaming the fish for the table), and what exactly do the sources say about whether any of it is working?

Verify: the year and place of first import and who did it; the escape routes and dates; how far upstream the fish have reached and whether they have entered the Great Lakes; any figures for share of river biomass or catch; the existence, location and type of any barrier; any documented injuries from jumping carp; the new market name and when it was adopted. Separate documented fact from legend: claims that the fish "rule" or have "taken over" a river must be scoped to the reaches the sources describe. No unsupported population estimates.

Storyboard: one recurring story object drawn the same way every time -- a pond-side fish crate / holding tank the carp arrive in, which returns at the close beside the opening person's water. One explanatory cutaway on the mechanism: a single underwater picture of the food web, carp gill-raker filtering of plankton with arrows showing the plankton no longer reaching the native filter-feeders and young fish, no text. No maps: show the spread as concrete things in real places -- a fish in a flooded pond spillway, a riverside boat ramp, a lock and dam -- not geography. People in frame: the pond keeper the viewer is asked to imagine stays visually consistent and supplies the viewpoint; documented people (biologists, fishers, boaters) look different and carry only their documented actions. Close by returning to the opening person's water and claim only an outcome the sources support.
