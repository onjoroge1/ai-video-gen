OPENING: the hook is the viewer's own guess about radium, posed as a guess and never as a claim about what
people believed: for example "You'd think radium was feared from the start." Use a myth line ONLY if a ledger
claim proves it wrong; if research does not support it, pick another assumption a claim does bust, or fall back
to the ordinary frame. Then: the bust (one cited sentence: the assumption is wrong, NOT yet why), the danger (one
cited sentence of what radium did to the women who worked with it), and the one open question the film answers
(for example "So what was it really doing to them?"). Then the viewer's role: a young woman taking a job painting
glowing numbers on watch dials, the need tangible (the pay, the glamour, the work) before any company name. Give
the decision (how the women were taught to paint, and what they were told about safety) its rationale as the
sources give it. Reach the first unintended consequence within about forty seconds of narration.

Causal arc (as questions for research to answer, not facts): How was radium marketed and sold to the public in the
1910s-1920s, and what health claims were made? Who painted luminous watch dials, where, for which companies, and
for what pay? How were the painters taught to shape their brushes, and what were they told about the paint's
safety? What happened to their health, and when did doctors connect it to the paint? What is the mechanism by which
swallowed radium harms the body (for example, whether the body treats it like calcium and deposits it in bone)? What
did the companies know and say? What legal cases followed, what was decided, and what changed afterwards in worker
safety or radiation standards?

STORY SPINE (which research fact fills which role; say each only as the sources state it):
- setup: the women hired to paint luminous watch dials, and the job itself (who, where, from when) -- an event or
  context claim about the work, NOT how the paint glows.
- false resolution: radium believed beneficial -- sold and talked about as good for the body.
- intervention (the decision): the painters taught to point their brushes with their lips, and told by their
  employers that radium was harmless or good for them.
- mechanism: why it harmed them -- radium taken up like calcium and deposited in bone (the answer to the opening
  question).
- hinge: the first illnesses among the painters.
- escalation: the illnesses and deaths that followed, and how the companies responded.
- reversal: the reckoning -- the 1928 New Jersey settlement and the 1938 Illinois Industrial Commission ruling, as
  sources state them.
- verdict: the conclusion those cases support, stated as a conclusion, not as another dated event.
How the paint glowed is background inside a scene, never the setup.

Research starting points (each checked 2026-10-12: it opens to an automated fetch and states the beat named):
- U.S. National Archives, "The Radium Girls at the National Archives"
  (https://text-message.blogs.archives.gov/2018/01/04/the-radium-girls-at-the-national-archives): how small amounts of
  radium were said to benefit the body; lip pointing; the calcium mechanism; the out-of-court settlements.
- Library of Congress, "Radium Girls: Living Dead Women" (https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2019/03/radium-girls-living-dead-women/):
  the 1928 New Jersey settlement.
- Oak Ridge Associated Universities, "Radium Girls: the health scandal of radium dial painters"
  (https://orau.org/blog/history/radium-girls-the-health-scandal-of-radium-dial-painters-in-the-1920s-and-1930s.html):
  instructors and what the painters were told about safety.
- Memorial University of Newfoundland, "Radium Watch-Dial Painters" (https://www.mun.ca/biology/scarr/Radium_Watch-Dial_Painters.html):
  radium as a bone seeker, chemically similar to calcium.
- Illinois Public Media, "The Radium Girls: An Illinois Tragedy"
  (https://ipm-prod.will.illinois.edu/news/story/illinois-issues-the-radium-girls-an-illinois-tragedy): the Ottawa painters and
  the 1938 Illinois Industrial Commission decision.
- ORAU Health Physics Museum, Radithor (https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/collection/radioactive-quack-cures/pills-potions-and-other-miscellany/radithor.html):
  radium sold as a health product.
