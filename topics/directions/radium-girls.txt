Bolt Explains the World: one sourced intervention backfire (an invention adopted that did the opposite of its
purpose), ~8-minute landscape illustrated documentary. MYTH-BUSTER OPENING (run with --opening myth).

OPENING: the hook is the viewer's own guess about radium, posed as a guess and never as a claim about what
people believed: for example "You'd think radium was feared from the start." Use a myth line ONLY if a ledger
claim proves it wrong; if research does not support it, pick another assumption a claim does bust, or fall back
to the ordinary frame. Then: the bust (one cited sentence: the assumption is wrong, NOT yet why), the danger (one
cited sentence of what radium did to the women who worked with it), and the one open question the film answers
(for example "So what was it really doing to them?"). Then the viewer's role: a young woman taking a job painting
glowing numbers on watch dials, the need tangible (the pay, the glamour, the work) before any company name. Give
the decision (how the women were taught to paint, and what they were told about safety) its rationale as the
sources give it. Reach the first unintended consequence within about forty seconds of narration.

Causal arc (as questions for research to answer, not facts): How was radium marketed and sold to the public in the
1910s-1920s, and what health claims were made? Who painted luminous watch dials, where, for which companies, and
for what pay? How were the painters taught to shape their brushes, and what were they told about the paint's
safety? What happened to their health, and when did doctors connect it to the paint? What is the mechanism by which
swallowed radium harms the body (for example, whether the body treats it like calcium and deposits it in bone)? What
did the companies know and say? What legal cases followed, what was decided, and what changed afterwards in worker
safety or radiation standards?

MECHANISM (the answer to the opening question): the documented biological reason radium harmed the women, in plain
words, stated as the answer: "It was never the glow. It was ..." -- only as research supports it.

Verify: the years of dial painting and of the first deaths; the companies and places (for example the U.S. Radium
Corporation in New Jersey and the Radium Dial Company in Illinois -- confirm or drop); the "lip, dip, paint"
practice; any count of workers or deaths; the court cases, their dates and outcomes; any standard or law that
followed. Separate documented fact from later retellings; do not dramatize any death beyond what a source states.

Research starting points: U.S. EPA Radionuclide Basics: Radium (https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radium);
CDC/ATSDR toxicological profile and ToxFAQs for radium (https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/); Oak Ridge Associated
Universities Health Physics Museum on radium products (https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/); the National
Library of Medicine / PubMed Central for peer-reviewed history of the dial painters (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/).

Storyboard: the one recurring story object is a single luminous watch dial with its numbers glowing, drawn the
same way every time; the mechanism cutaway shows, in one picture with arrows and no text, radium taking calcium's
place in bone if research supports it; no maps; people in frame; the viewpoint dial painter visually consistent;
documented people look different from one another and carry only documented actions. No gore and no depiction of
injuries beyond what a source states; illness is shown through the people and their surroundings, not wounds.
